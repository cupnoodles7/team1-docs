Section 1

Context & flow of the application

What the app does, who builds which part, and how a request travels from a tap to a rendered button.

What we are building

The T&F Reader is a React Native app that puts Taylor & Francis books, journals, articles and audiobooks behind one interface. A reader finds their institution, signs in through it, and browses the catalogue that institution is entitled to. Some titles are free to read, some need a licence generated the first time they are opened, and some have a limited number of licences to go round.

Four teams of five build it over eight weeks. team1 owns discovery and selection — finding an institution, choosing one, and the catalogue browse, search and detail screens that follow. team1's own plan runs to four weeks, feature-complete at the end of Week 3, with Week 4 for integration.

Deliberately not used

No NativeWind or styled-components — tokens are the only styling vocabulary. No Redux — global state is three slices, and the complex logic is a pure function. No axios — we follow URLs we were handed, so fetch plus one wrapper is enough. No prop-types — React 19 ignores it.

Architecture — four layers, one rule each

screens/ + features/     may fetch, navigate, hold local state
components/              props in, callbacks out. Nothing else
adapters/ + model/       the only place that knows wokay's shapes
access/                  the only place access logic may live
The rule that has already paid for itself three times

A component receives data and renders it. It does not fetch, read a store, navigate, or work out access rights. ContentCard takes item and access as props and computes nothing. wokay's contract changed shape three times in two weeks — the access matrix inverted, the tier stopped being a field, the action rule moved — and the card needed no edit on any of them. A card that read accessTier and picked its own button would have been rework each time.

The data contract

TF_Reader_Mobile_team1/src/model/types.ts is the contract. One file, four people code against it, and it is what goes to wokay and flambeau for correction. Changes land there first and everything follows.

Five fields are worked out, not read

This is where the normalisation risk lives. All five are one-way doors: get them wrong and the mistake shows up on a screen, not in a test.

FieldHow it is obtained
Access tierFrom the acquisition link's licenceModel. UNLIMITED → SUBSCRIPTION · CONCURRENT → ELITE · key absent entirely → OPEN_ACCESS. wokay's own mapping. There is no tier field.
File formatFrom the acquisition link's MIME. application/pdf → PDF, application/epub+zip → EPUB, anything under audio/ → AUDIO. There is no contentType in the feed.
Work typeFrom metadata.@type. schema.org/Book and schema.org/Audiobook confirmed; journal and article values still to come.
Item idFrom the tail of the publication's own self URL. metadata.identifier is not the id, and the batch endpoint needs the real one.
ISBNUnwrapped from metadata.identifier — urn:isbn:9780367211745. Titles without one carry urn:tf:catalogue:… instead.
The subtle one

"Looked it up and got nothing" and "the key is not there" are different facts. A missing licenceModel means Open Access. Treat the two cases the same and every Subscription title with a slightly malformed link renders as free to read. The derivation takes the whole properties object, not a string, so the absent case cannot be lost at the call site.

All five derivations happen in the adapter. By the time data reaches a component, accessTier is a plain value again — which is what keeps the design rule intact.

Two things that closed, and one that did not
  • DOI is gone for good. There is no DOI field and there never will be — only wokay's own identifier. Drop DOI from screen 04. A design change to make, not an answer to wait for.
  • One format per title. A publication has exactly one acquisition link. Consequence: FormatSelector has nothing to select between — decide whether to drop it or keep it as a single-format display strip.
  • B2C is retained. wokay recommended cutting individual subscribers at their own gate; that recommendation is not being taken and they will supply the details later. So subscribe stays in the action vocabulary, Session.type stays, and screen 03's second option is reopened rather than settled.
  • The action vocabulary is still six words, but not the same six. borrow is gone; revokeLicence takes its place. The set is read · download · addToQueue · revokeLicence · subscribe · signIn. ActionButton keeps its six variants and its props do not change — which is the whole reason a flow change this size costs the component library nothing.

The institution endpoints land first, and they matter most. Keshav is the first person who can delete a mock. His work is also on the sign-in critical path: if catalogue search breaks, a reader browses instead; if institution search breaks, nobody can sign in, obtain entitlements, or read anything. Low difficulty, high consequence — give it review attention rather than help.

Four rules wokay set, which the method signatures enforce
  1. Follow URLs, never build them. Every catalogue method takes a URL we were handed. The only string we ever construct is expanding the search template …/search{?query}.
  2. Page by following the next link until it is absent. Never count pages.
  3. An empty result is not an empty array. A zero-result search returns a feed containing a navigation entry pointing back at the catalogue. Render it as a browse-instead button; a missing publications key is not an error.
  4. The acquisition link decides the buttons — not our logic. No link means no button.
Navigation rows and shelves are two different lists

The root feed carries both, and they do not match. In wokay's own words: "one request draws the home screen: navigation rows and shelves."

In the feedSample contentsWhere it renders
navigation (3)eBooks · Audiobooks · Open access — pointers, no itemsThe tabs
groups (2)New this term · Free to read — each with inline publicationsScreen 01's sections, rendered directly with no second request

They can overlap: in the sample, one navigation row and one group point at the same URL under different names. Dedupe by URL or the same shelf appears twice.

Two pagination models, split cleanly. OPDS feeds use next links only; the institutions endpoint uses page/size/total. They belong to different endpoint families, so build both plainly rather than abstracting over them. Note that shelves and search do carry a result count (metadata.numberOfItems), so "3 results" is available even though page numbers are not.

Access resolution — the spine

resolveAccess(item, session, licence, availability) lives in src/access/ and is the only place access logic may exist. It interprets the feed; it is not a rules engine.

open access                     → read, download        no licence, ever
subscription, no licence yet   → read, download        the tap generates one
subscription, licence held    → read, download        checked before we open
elite, licence held           → read, download, revoke licence
elite, no licence, seat free  → read, download        the tap generates one
elite, no seat free           → add me to queue       detail screen only
no acquisition link             → nothing at all

canPersist: false               → download hidden, whatever the row above says
  • session decides signed-in versus needs-sign-in. Open Access resolves the same either way.
  • licence decides whether we generate one on the tap or check the one already held. It also decides whether Elite shows Revoke licence.
  • availability decides no-seats — ELITE only, detail screen only, because the number of free licences is not in the feed.
  • tier decides the badge and nothing else.

availability is null on every list surface. That is why a ContentCard can never show a no-seats state — structural, rather than something a reviewer has to remember.

The button set barely changes. What sits behind it does.

This is the change that matters most, and it is easy to under-read: Read and Download are now the front door for every tier. There is no Borrow step and no second screen state to get through first. A reader opens a title and sees the two buttons they expect. The licence work happens behind them.

TierFirst openEvery open afterWhat we do
OPEN ACCESS Read · Download Read · Download No licence and no check, at any point. Same signed in or out. The download is theirs to keep. flambeau is never called.
SUBSCRIPTION Read · Download Read · Download The first tap generates a licence through flambeau, then opens. Every later tap passes a licence check to flambeau first, and opens once it comes back good.
ELITE Read · Download Read · Download · Revoke licence Same generate-then-check as Subscription, but licences are limited. Revoke licence hands theirs back so the next reader in the queue can have it.
ELITE — no licences free Add me to queue Detail screen only, because availability is null in every list. They are notified in the app when their turn comes.
No entitlementNothing at allNo button, no gate, no message. See below.
Two taps that look identical and are not

First tap and every tap after it show the same button, in the same place, with the same label. The reader is not meant to be able to tell them apart. But one is a write — generate a licence — and the other is a read — check a licence. They fail differently, they take different amounts of time, and only one of them can be turned down because the shelf is empty.

So which of the two we are about to do is decided in resolveAccess, not at the button. The button is handed an action to perform and performs it. Keeping that decision in one place is the only reason the same ActionButton can serve all three tiers.

The queue, and the notification that comes out of it

Elite titles have a fixed number of licences. When they are all out, the only thing on offer is Add me to queue. Later — when somebody reads to the end, revokes, or their licence lapses — a licence frees up and flambeau works out whose turn it is.

At that point a notification appears inside the app, with two choices: Accept and Reject.

  • Accept — the licence is generated for them and the title opens as normal. From here it behaves exactly like any other Elite title they hold.
  • Reject — they give up the place, the licence goes to whoever is next, and the title falls back to Add me to queue.
  • Neither — an offer that is never answered has to expire, or one silent reader stalls the whole queue. flambeau owns the timeout; we have not been given the length
Three things about the notification we do not yet know

How it reaches us. Somebody has to tell the app a licence is waiting. Push, a socket, or the app polling flambeau while it is open are all different amounts of work, and only the last one is entirely ours. Ask flambeau in Week 1 — this is the answer that decides how big the job is.

Where it appears. No mockup exists for it in the eighteen screens. It has to be visible from wherever the reader happens to be, so it is an in-app banner or sheet rather than a screen of its own.

What happens when the app is shut. If the offer arrives and expires while the app is closed, the reader loses their place having never seen it. That is a real product decision, not an edge case — raise it rather than picking an answer in code.

Why the no-entitlement state is nearly dead code in B2B

The signed-in feed is scoped by wokay to what the institution is entitled to, so a B2B reader should never reach a title they cannot act on. resolveAccess still has to return the empty case — a malformed link or a stale deep link can produce it — but it renders as no buttons, not as an error or an upsell. Do not invent a "request access" affordance for it; there is no endpoint behind one.

When the resolve cannot be trusted

If the detail fetch or the availability call fails or times out, fall back to Read disabled with a retry — never to Download. Guessing towards the more generous button hands an unentitled reader a file. The three states are distinct and must look distinct on screen: loading (buttons skeletoned), failed (retry), resolved to nothing (no buttons).

A licence check that fails is not permission to open the title. Same rule, one step later: if flambeau cannot be reached on the tap, the title does not open and the reader gets a retry. Falling through to the file because the check timed out is the worst possible failure here, and it is the easy mistake to write.

Licence work happens on the tap, not on render — which is what keeps lists cheap. Tier and links come down with the feed, so a list of forty cards draws forty pairs of buttons with no extra calls at all. Only two things are separate requests, and both are detail-screen only: the Elite no-seats check, and whatever the reader's tap sets off.

Every access-bearing request carries the chosen institution. The same publication resolves differently for two institutions, so a cached resolve is keyed on institution id, not just item id. If no institution is selected there is no signed-in feed to be on, and the reader belongs in the picker.

Hiding Download is a UI decision, not a protection — the download endpoint enforces entitlement itself. That is wokay's and flambeau's side, noted here so nobody assumes the button is the control.

Building against an unratified correction

The signed design specification's access matrix is now two revisions behind, and neither correction is ratified. It first disagreed with wokay on two of the three tiers; the table above then replaced Borrow entirely with generate-on-first-tap, check-on-every-tap-after. Anyone working from the signed spec builds a Borrow button that no longer exists. The table above is the one to build.

Elite and Download is the open question inside the new flow. The old rule was that Elite never offers a Download; the new flow gives every tier Read and Download and adds two buttons on top for Elite. The table above follows the new flow. If Elite is meant to stay read-only, it is a one-line change in resolveAccess and nothing else moves — but get it confirmed before the demo, because it is the difference between a limited copy staying on the shelf and going home on a device.

And there is a field in the feed that now disagrees with us. The acquisition link carries canPersist, which is wokay saying whether a title may be kept on the device. Showing Download on every tier means we either ignore that field or let it override the table above. Ignoring a field wokay put there on purpose is not a decision to make quietly — ask them what it is for now, and until they answer, honour it: canPersist: false hides Download whatever the tier.

Two rules: no server data in a store, and no resolved access in a store. A cached access result is how a stale button outlives the licence it was drawn from.

The queue offer is the one exception, and it is deliberate. It sits in a store because the reader has to be able to answer it from anywhere in the app, and it persists because an offer that vanishes when the app is backgrounded is worse than no offer at all. Whether flambeau still honours it after a restart is theirs to answer — we hold it, we re-confirm before acting on it, and we never assume it is still good.

Wire this while the navigator is open

Persisted state rehydrates asynchronously, so the chosen institution is briefly absent on a cold start. The root navigator must wait for rehydration before choosing between the institution picker and the catalogue, or a returning reader sees the picker flash on every launch. Zustand exposes persist.hasHydrated() for exactly this.

Errors

wokay send one error shape for every failure, carrying an enumerated code. Copy is keyed on that code, never on the HTTP status — so a reader sees "your library's subscription has expired" rather than "not available".

UNAUTHENTICATED · TOKEN_EXPIRED · FORBIDDEN_INSTITUTION_MISMATCH · NO_ENTITLEMENT · ENTITLEMENT_EXPIRED · ENTITLEMENT_SUSPENDED · CONTENT_NOT_READY · DOWNLOAD_NOT_PERMITTED · INVALID_DEVICE_PUBLIC_KEY · NOT_FOUND

Nothing in that list covers a licence. Generating one can fail because there are none left; a check can fail because the licence expired, was revoked elsewhere, or was issued to another device. Those are four different sentences on screen and there is no code for any of them yet — ask flambeau for their enumeration in Week 1, and until it arrives keep licence failures behind one honest fallback message rather than inventing codes we will have to rename.

ErrorState takes a string, never an Error object — no stack traces reach a screen. A publication the institution is not entitled to returns 404, identical to one that does not exist, so the catalogue cannot be probed by guessing ids. The same applies to inactive institutions: treat 404 on institution detail as not-found, never as forbidden.

Decisions on record

Recorded so they read as decisions rather than as things nobody noticed.

  1. No per-institution theming in the prototype. One fixed palette; branding.primaryColor is carried and deliberately unused, so adopting it later is additive rather than a refactor.
  2. The tier drives the badge only — never an action. Confirmed to wokay in writing.
  3. Sort institutions alphabetically server-side; recently-used pinning is ours and client-side.
  4. Yes to subject facets — asked for explicitly, because silence would have removed the feature.
  5. The public open-access feed is a skip-institution path, and an addition rather than a replacement now that B2C is retained.
  6. Screen 08 is t4targaryen's. Screens 07 and 08 belong together.
  7. DOI is removed from screen 04.
  8. TypeScript with strict: true, reversing the earlier JavaScript call. any and as allowed without review pushback in Week 1 — the value is in the contract file and the props, not in purity.
  9. Borrow is removed as a step the reader has to take. Read and Download are the front door for every tier. The licence is generated by the first tap and checked on every tap after it, and the reader is never shown the difference.
  10. Open Access never touches flambeau. No licence is generated, none is checked, and no call is made — not even a cheap one. It is the only tier where the tap goes straight to the file.
  11. Revoke licence is Elite only, and only appears once a licence is actually held. Handing back a seat matters where seats are countable; on Subscription it would be a button that costs the reader something and gains them nothing.
  12. A queue offer is answered inside the app, with Accept and Reject. Not an email, not a deep link — the reader is already where the title is.

The Day 2 gate is absolute

No feature branch is cut until the foundation is merged and the app runs on a device or simulator — confirmed by all five people on their own machines, not just the author's. A scaffold that only runs on one laptop is not a scaffold. Two days of apparent slowness buys back a week; skipping the gate is what turns Week 3 integration into a rewrite.

If Day 2 evening is not green, cut the core five to three — ContentCard, TopAppBar, Skeleton — rather than cutting the contract or the fixtures.

The licence flow change does not move Week 1 — and that is worth saying out loud

Week 1 above is unchanged, deliberately. Every day of it is scaffold, tokens, contract, fixtures and components, and not one of those twenty-one components knows what a licence is. ActionButton still renders a label and fires a callback; ActionBar still lays out however many it is given. The flow change lands in resolveAccess and the flambeau calls behind it, and both of those are Week 2.

Two small things to carry into the week rather than re-plan around it. Akriti's Day 1 contract file uses the new action vocabulary, so nothing gets written with a borrow in it that has to be renamed later. And the flambeau questions on the licence calls and the queue notification go out with Day 1's four question sets, because the answers set the size of Week 2.

The two genuine fan-out risks in Week 1

With the library split five ways, only two artefacts block more than one person. Section 3

GitHub flow

Applies to all three repos and all four teams. Anything marked assumed is a default chosen so the process is usable — confirm or overwrite it.

Repos and forks

Two client surfaces, three repos.

ClientRepoStack

Serves bothtf_reader_backendSpring Boot modular monolith + MongoDB + Redis

Each upstream repo has exactly two long-lived branches. main is production — live, protected, never pushed to directly. stage is UAT and the integration point for all four teams. There is no develop.

Each team's lead forks; everyone else clones the team fork. Nobody clones upstream directly. Four forks per repo, twelve in total.

          tf_reader_mobile_app          ← upstream, org-owned
          ├── main   (prod)
          └── stage  (UAT)
                   ▲
   ┌─────────┬─────┴─────┬────────────┐
  b1        b2          b3           b4        ← team forks
(team1)   (wokay)   (flambeau) (t4targaryen)

The single most confusing part of this setup

In a fork, main is the team's dev integration line — not production. It deploys to the team dev environment. stage is unused in forks; do not create or track it there. State this in every fork's README, as ours does.

Remotes on every dev's clone
git clone git@github.com:<lead>/tf_reader_mobile_app.git   # this is b1
cd tf_reader_mobile_app
git remote add upstream git@github.com:<org>/tf_reader_mobile_app.git

# origin    b1  (fetch/push)   ← your team fork
# upstream  b   (fetch)        ← the real repo, read-only for you

Only the team lead ever pushes to upstream, and only via PR.

Branch naming

feature/CAP-<n>-<slug>     feature/CAP-2-institution-list
fix/CAP-<n>-<slug>         fix/CAP-3-selection-persist
chore/<slug>               chore/ci-cache-gradle
spike/<slug>               spike/opds-parser
release/<yyyy-mm-dd>       release/2026-08-13   (stage → main only)
hotfix/<slug>              hotfix/jwt-expiry    (branches off main)

Lowercase, hyphens, no personal names in branch names.

The daily loop

# 1 · start of every session — sync down
git checkout main && git pull --ff-only origin main
git fetch upstream

# 2 · create the feature branch
git checkout -b feature/CAP-2-institution-list

# 3 · while working — rebase, never merge
git checkout main && git pull --ff-only origin main
git checkout - && git rebase main

# 4 · lead only, every 2–3 days — sync the fork with upstream
git checkout main && git fetch upstream
git rebase upstream/stage      # forks track upstream STAGE, not main
git push origin main

Forks rebase onto upstream stage, not main, because stage is where the other three teams' work lands. Rebasing onto main would leave you a week behind. Rebase rather than merge so history stays linear and the weekly upstream PR stays reviewable.

Push cadence

Push your feature branch to the fork every 2–3 days minimum, even if incomplete — prefix the PR Draft: if it is not ready. This is a hackathon-speed build, and a branch that lives five days unpushed is a conflict grenade. Our own team rule is tighter still: daily merge to main, and no branch lives longer than one day. Long branches plus a shared component library is the worst combination available to this team.

Pre-PR checklist, and review

Run all of this before raising the PR:

  • Rebased on the latest fork main
  • Tests pass locally, and npm run typecheck and npm run lint are clean
  • Read your own diff hunk by hunk — catch stray debug logs, commented-out blocks, a committed .env, accidental formatting churn
  • A README note if the feature warrants one — new module, new env var, non-obvious setup
  • AI pass for optimisation, regression risk and code quality
  • Security review on the diff — /security-review, every PR, not just auth or crypto work
  • No secrets, no tokens, no real institution or user data in the diff

The PR goes to the team fork, never upstream: feature/CAP-2-institution-list → b1:main. It must say what changed, why, how to test, and link the CAP id, with screenshots for UI.

Merging
  • Reviewers auto-requested via CODEOWNERS — that file is the mechanism; CODE_REVIEW.md and CONTRIBUTORS.md are the documentation
  • Minimum one approval from someone who is not the author. If you wrote the component being extended, you are a required reviewer.
  • CI green, squash merge, delete the branch
  • Reviews turned around within one working day; Thursday PRs same day
  • A component merges before the screen that consumes it. Changes to model/, adapters/, access/ or theme/ need the named owner.

The one hard rule

If a team's fork main is red on Thursday, that team does not raise a PR that week. A broken stage blocks twenty people.

Promotion to production is not part of the weekly loop. It is a deliberate release: cut release/<date> from stage once it has been green and smoke-tested, requiring platform owner plus one lead, a manual approval gate on the prod deploy, and a version tag on merge.

CI/CD and environments

The cloud target is not decided, so the pipelines are provider-agnostic. Every repo has one script, .ci/deploy.sh, always called the same way:

.ci/deploy.sh --env "$ENV_NAME" --image "$IMAGE_REF" --ref "$GITHUB_SHA"

The workflow contract is fixed and does not change when the cloud is chosen — only deploy.sh changes. Today it can be a stub that echoes and exits 0. Images go to GitHub Container Registry, which works everywhere and any cloud can pull from later.

Six environments per repo — prod, stage, and one dev per team — giving eighteen in total, plus every developer's local machine. Each gets its own MongoDB database and Redis namespace; never point a dev environment at stage data, and seed from a checked-in fixture set rather than a prod dump.

Two things that catch people out

Forks run their own CI. Actions in a fork use the fork's billing and the fork's secrets — upstream secrets are never available to a fork. That is a GitHub security boundary, not a misconfiguration, so each of the twelve forks needs its own secret set. Team leads own this in Week 1.

macOS runners bill at 10× Linux minutes. Run the iOS job only on PRs to stage/main and on a nightly schedule, not on every fork push, or four teams will burn the org's quota by Week 2.

What "deploy" means for the mobile app assumed
EnvWhat CI produces

This needs no signing certs for dev or stage and no store accounts. When store distribution is needed, deploy.sh gains a Firebase App Distribution or TestFlight step and the workflow does not change.

Branch protection, secrets, hotfixes

Upstream main
  • No direct or force pushes; source must be stage or hotfix/*
  • Two approvals, one a CODEOWNER
  • Required checks; stale approvals dismissed on new commits
  • Linear history; administrators included
Upstream stage
  • PR required, one CODEOWNER approval, same checks, linear history
Team forks main
  • PR required even for the lead; one approval, not the author
  • Linear history — this is what enforces the rebase policy
Secrets

Never in git: .env, keystores, google-services.json, signing keys, connection strings. Every repo commits a .env.example with keys only, no values, and gitleaks in CI catches slips.

Config precedence: repo defaults → env-specific file → environment variables from CI secrets. If it differs between the eighteen environments, it is an env var. Prod secrets are known to platform owners only — no team lead needs them.

Hotfixes — the only path that bypasses the weekly rhythm
  1. Branch hotfix/* off upstream main, minimal fix plus a test
  2. PR to main: platform owner + one lead, CI green, deploy through the approval gate
  3. Immediately re-apply the same fix to stage. A hotfix that only exists on main gets reverted by the next release.
Still needing a decision

Cloud target · whether artifact-only mobile deploys are acceptable · who owns platform and CI · the team-to-fork mapping · stage → main cadence · Gradle or Maven · whether twelve always-on dev environments are really warranted for an eight-week build · and where the shared API contract lives so mobile and backend do not drift.

Values inferred rather than specified — flag these, do not treat them as ratified design

radius.card: 8 and radius.sheet: 16 are specified. But radius.pill is inferred (the spec says only "pill-shaped"), the whole space scale is inferred (no spacing scale exists anywhere in the design specification), and elevation.card is inferred from "subtle box shadow" with no values given. React Native splits shadows by platform, so it has to be a token or five authors invent five shadows.

There is also no dark palette. The spec mentions navy for "dark mode overlays" but defines nothing else. Week 1 is light only — raise it if dark mode is expected at the demo.

Component library — 21 components, plus one added in Week 2

One component, one folder, three files: the component, its gallery entry, and a re-export. Props are declared once, as one exported interface; where the contract already defines a union, import the type rather than retyping the literals.

The five rules everyone signs up to
  1. Props in, callbacks out. A component receives data and emits events. It does not fetch, read a store, navigate, or compute access.
  2. Naming. Visual variations are variant; lifecycle is state; handlers are on<Event>. No component invents a fourth vocabulary. Unions, never booleans — two booleans allow a fourth state that has no design.
  3. No raw values. Every colour, size, spacing and radius comes from the theme. A raw hex in src/components/ fails review.
  4. States are built in, not added later. Anything that can load, be empty, error or go offline handles those from the first commit. Retrofitting states in Week 3 changes the prop signature, so every caller changes too.
  5. A feature may not introduce a component. If a feature needs something the library lacks, it is added to the library, in the library's files, reviewed by the component's original author. Never inside a feature folder. The rule being protected is one implementation, one location — and this is the rule most likely to break quietly under deadline pressure.

The gallery is a hidden route rendering every component in every variant on one scrolling screen, from static props with no provider, store, network or navigation container. It is the Friday review surface and the Week 3 state-matrix surface. A component is not done until its gallery entry renders every state it claims to support.

What the Friday review looks for

All twenty-one side by side: divergent spacing, off-token colours, inconsistent prop naming, missing state variants, two components that should be one. Plus the two things that compile cleanly and still drift — any in a props interface, and a union retyped as literals instead of imported from the contract. Anything found is fixed before Monday, not logged.

All 18 screens

Click any mockup to open it full size. Screens 07, 08 and 13 are not team1's, and are included because we hand off to them or share a shell with them.

The rules that bind design to the build

  • The UI must never calculate access rights. Every screen renders from the output of resolveAccess. This is what makes swapping the mock resolver for the real one a zero-UI-change operation — and it is why three contract reversals cost us nothing in the component library.
  • Skeletons replace spinners. Rectangular placeholders at the exact dimensions of the final content, so nothing jumps. A hard design rule, not a preference.
  • Cards are invariant across scopes. The card, badge and layout are identical in every scope; only the set of items and the resolved actions change.
  • The reader is never shown the licence machinery. No "generating licence", no "checking licence", no licence id on a screen. They tap Read and the title opens, or it does not and they are told why in plain words. The tier badge is the only place any of this surfaces at all.
  • Every action button has a busy state. Read, Download, Add me to queue and Revoke licence can all sit waiting on flambeau. A button that looks idle while a request is in flight gets tapped again, and on Revoke that is a second request against something already gone.
  • Errors are plain text with Retry or Learn more. No stack traces, and ErrorState takes a string rather than an Error.
  • Offline means degraded but usable. The library and the institution list keep working behind the banner.
  • One fixed palette. No per-institution theming in the prototype — a runtime-variable primary colour means no token is a constant, and the consistency review would have no fixed baseline to review against.
Where the design specification is silent

No spacing scale, no pill radius value, no shadow values, no dark palette, and no institution model at all — OPDS does not model institutions and neither does the design spec, so that shape is entirely ours and is the one we sent to wokay for correction.

Add to that list the queue notification. There is no mockup, no copy for Accept and Reject, and no decision on whether it is a banner at the top or a sheet from the bottom. Amber and the existing banner pattern get us a defensible first version, but it is our invention until design says otherwise — show it to them in Week 2, not at the demo.
