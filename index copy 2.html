01

Executive Summary

The T&F Knowledge Hub is the access-control spine plus the reading surface for Taylor & Francis academic content, delivered as a Spring Cloud microservices backend and a React Native app.

The platform is B2B: it is sold to organizations (universities and libraries) on subscription. There is no consumer purchase path — but any logged-in user, even one not attached to an organization, can browse the entire catalogue and read open-access content.

The MVP proves one loop end to end: log in → browse everything → get gated at read/download → read & track. Every user sees the whole catalogue. Open-access content opens for anyone. For licensed (non-open-access) content, two time-bounded checks fire at the moment of reading or download — License (does one of the user's organizations license this title?) and Entitlement (is the user an active member of that organization?). Once reading, the app tracks progress, highlights, bookmarks, and downloads, online and offline.

The one-sentence mental model

Everyone who logs in sees all content. Open-access opens for anyone. For licensed content, access is granted only if one of the user's organizations licenses it (License) and the user is an active member of that organization (Entitlement) — evaluated when they try to read or download, not while browsing.

Non-open-access files are encrypted at rest with AES-256-GCM and unlocked by our own License layer — a practical, free stand-in for the paid Readium LCP — while reading is rendered in the free, open-source Readium toolkit. Operations and business KPIs are tracked from day one with Prometheus + Grafana, woven into every service like logging.

02

Problem & Opportunity

Academic readers work across fragmented surfaces: books in one system, journals and their articles in another, each with its own access rules, logins, and reading experience. Institutions pay for large subscriptions but struggle to give members a single, coherent, offline-capable place to read what they are entitled to.

The opportunity is a unified, single-publisher reading platform for Taylor & Francis content that:

  • presents all content to every user (discovery is never blocked), while enforcing entitlement precisely at the point of consumption;
  • models the institutional reality — organizations hold subscriptions, users belong to organizations, and access follows from both;
  • protects commercial content without the cost and gating of a full commercial DRM stack;
  • gives the business/sales team a live view of engagement and — critically — unmet demand (what users tried to open but couldn't).
Scope discipline

The PRD is the destination; this document specifies phase one. It reconciles the full product vision with a deliberately narrowed MVP, and states explicitly what is deferred and what is dropped (see Scope and Roadmap).

03

Background & Inspiration

The architecture is inspired by The Palace Project — an open-source, cloud-based digital library platform operated by Lyrasis with the Digital Public Library of America, and the successor to NYPL's Library Simplified / SimplyE. We studied it to ground our design in a proven, standards-based model for institutional e-content lending.

◎Open standards

Palace is glued together by OPDS catalogue feeds and the Readium reading toolkit. We adopt OPDS 2.0 feeds and the free Readium toolkit for the same reasons.

◲Distributed services

A backend of independently deployable services talking over feeds — mirrored here as Spring Cloud microservices with a database-per-service.

⌸DRM as a spectrum

Palace ships Readium LCP and Adobe DRM (off by default). This validated our choice to reproduce LCP's shape with free primitives rather than pay for the certified stack in the MVP.

Key difference: Palace is a multi-publisher public-library lending network; the T&F Knowledge Hub is a single-publisher, B2B platform rebuilt for institutional subscriptions. Full research notes live in README.md.

07

Product Overview

The product is the access-control spine plus the reading surface. Licensing + entitlement is the hard, differentiating core; reading + sync is the engagement surface. Everything else layers on top of a correct access model.

The loop

  1. A user logs in (JWT) and sees the entire catalogue.
  2. Open-access content is readable/downloadable by anyone.
  3. For licensed content, two time-bounded checks fire at read/download time — License (from the org's licenseId[]) and Entitlement (from the user's entitlementId[]).
  4. Once reading, the app tracks progress, highlights, bookmarks, and downloads (offline-capable, synced).
Design principle

Discovery is never gated — the gate is at consumption. The whole catalogue is visible to everyone; enforcement happens only when a user tries to read online or download.

08

The Access Model

Access is expressed as two orthogonal, time-bounded records — a license the org holds and an entitlement the user holds. They answer different questions and are kept deliberately separate.

License org ↔ content

Does one of the user's organizations license this title? A thin licenses doc ({isbn, start, end, openAccess}) the org holds via organization.licenseId[], matched on the content's isbn. For an article, its parent journal's license.

Entitlement user ↔ access

Does the user hold a valid entitlements doc ({start, end}) via user.entitlementId[], and is the user active in the org's userList[]? The "who you are" layer.

Why they are separate — the 2×2

The two dimensions are independent: an organization can license a title without a given user being an active member, and a user can be an active member of an org that hasn't licensed a given title. Only the intersection grants access.

Library-card analogy

License is whether the library owns the book. Entitlement is whether your library card is valid. You need both to check the book out — and each is owned and revoked independently, which is exactly why they are two collections owned by two services.

Rules

  • Open access (catalogue.openAccess / article.openAccess) → skip both checks, serve the file plainly.
  • Multi-org resolution — the first qualifying org (holds a matching valid license + subscription valid + user active) wins.
  • Validity window — a license/entitlement is honored only while start ≤ now ≤ end (bounded by subscription.endsAt); expired → deny. The gate reads the reference arrays — no per-user materialization, no cron.

10

Non-Functional Requirements

Security

JWT validated at the edge; content encrypted at rest; keys never embedded in a signed token; /actuator endpoints internal-only; Grafana behind auth.

Isolation

Database-per-service — services never share a database; they call one another via the Gateway / Eureka. Clear ownership boundaries.

Performance

Denormalized catalogue so list/search hit one collection with no join; short-TTL signed URLs; lazy grant refresh avoids a reconciliation cron.

Portability & ops

Stateless services behind the Gateway; central config; Dockerized local stack; metrics/health from day one, added like logging.

Offline-first

Reading state is created offline and synced on reconnect; plaintext is never written to disk; keys live in the OS secure enclave.

Observability boundary

Metrics use bounded labels only — never userId/contentId — to protect Prometheus cardinality.

System Architecture

A single backend of independently deployable Spring Boot services, coordinated by Spring Cloud. The React Native app talks only to the API Gateway; the Gateway validates the JWT and routes to domain services, which discover each other through Eureka and read config from the Config Server. Each service owns its own MongoDB database. Content files live in S3 — open-access files are served plainly; non-open-access files are unlocked by a license token + key from the License service.

Why separate services

Each of Organization, License, and Entitlement is its own bounded context with a single responsibility, its own database, and an independent deploy/scale path — clean ownership boundaries that mirror the domain (who the tenant is · what is licensed · who is entitled). The read/download gate lives in the License service, which chains synchronous calls to Organization (subscription + membership) and Entitlement (user validity) — the accepted cost of keeping the contexts separate. "University" is generalized to Organization (its own service); user stays in Auth.

Data-ownership note (inverted references, separate services)

Ownership is inverted but the collections live in three separate services. A licenses doc ({isbn, start, end, openAccess}, in License) carries no userId/orgId — the organization references it via licenseId[] (in Organization); an entitlements doc (in Entitlement) likewise — the user references it via entitlementId[]. The access token carries the user's organizationId[] + entitlementId[]; the gate (in License) resolves those id-lists by calling Organization and Entitlement — a cross-service chain that is the accepted cost of separate bounded contexts. Arrays get long by design — an accepted requirement.

End-to-End Access Flow

The gate is at read/download time, not at browse time.

  1. Authenticate. Login (+ deviceId) → Auth creates a session and returns an access token (short-lived JWT, sent as Bearer) + a refresh token (kept in Keychain/Keystore).
  2. Browse everything. Catalog returns the full catalogue (OPDS feed), each item tagged with an access badge. No content is hidden.
  3. Open or download a title. The user tries to read online or download a book, or an article inside a journal.
  4. Open-access shortcut. If openAccess → serve plainly via a signed S3 URL and record in user_content.
  5. License check (License service, the gate — token carries organizationId[]). The gate calls Organization for the user's orgs that have a valid subscription with the user active and their licenseId[], then finds the first of those licenses whose isbn matches the content and is within start ≤ now ≤ end.
  6. Entitlement check (Entitlement service — token carries entitlementId[]). The gate calls Entitlement: those ids must resolve to a valid entitlement (entitlements, start ≤ now ≤ end).
  7. Unlock or deny. Both valid → License issues the unlock token + content key (over TLS); the encrypted file is fetched from S3 and decrypted in memory. Otherwise → deny (with reason).
  8. Read & track. Progress / highlights / bookmarks / downloads sync to user_content (last-write-wins).
  9. Access window. Access persists while the referenced licenses / entitlements are within start/end; each read re-runs the gate (License → Organization + Entitlement) against the current windows.
  10. Session lifecycle & device cap. On a 401 the client interceptor silently calls /auth/refresh and retries — no surprise logout; manage devices via GET /auth/sessions + logout / logout-all; sessions auto-expire via a TTL index. An account holds at most N devices (default 3) — a login beyond the cap is refused (device_limit_reached) until one is signed out.

Content Protection & Encryption

Non-open-access content is protected with an LCP-style scheme built from free, standard primitives — it mirrors the shape of Readium LCP without the paid EDRLab licensing. Every claim below is backed by a cited fact in ENCRYPTION.md.

The scheme

Encrypt at rest

Each non-open-access file is encrypted with AES-256-GCM (AEAD — confidentiality + integrity), a per-file random content key and a unique 96-bit IV. S3 stores ciphertext + IV + auth tag.

Gate the key

After License + Entitlement pass, the server returns a short-TTL presigned URL for the encrypted blob, a license JWS (validity window + deviceId), and the content key over TLS.

Never in the token

The key is never placed in a signed JWT — a JWS payload is base64url, readable by anyone. It travels over TLS (or, later, as a JWE).

Decrypt in memory

The app decrypts in memory, verifies the GCM tag, and hands a plain file to Readium / react-native-pdf. Plaintext never touches disk.

Key hierarchy & KMS — what exists before /access

A per-file content key (CEK) encrypts each file; the CEK is stored wrapped by a long-lived master key (KEK) on the content record; the master key lives in the secret store / Config Server (MVP) or AWS KMS HSM (production, rotatable). All keys exist before /access — the master key is needed at ingest to encrypt. /access doesn't create a key; it unwraps the CEK server-side and releases the plaintext CEK to an authorized client over TLS (never in S3, never in the JWS). We use app-layer encryption, not S3 SSE — otherwise a presigned URL would return plaintext. Full detail: ENCRYPTION.md §3.2.

Hardening

In the MVP

Unique-IV AES-GCM with AAD bound to {contentId, userId, deviceId}; content key over TLS; offline key in Keychain/Keystore; deviceId-bound license with short exp + silent heartbeat; server-side master key wraps per-file keys.

Deferred

AWS KMS envelope encryption; JWE key delivery + TLS pinning; device attestation (App Attest / Play Integrity); watermarking (social DRM); and real Readium LCP — the certified endpoint of this path.

Free-Readium consequence

Because the free reader consumes a plain file, we decrypt the whole publication in memory before handing it over — not per-resource like real LCP. react-native-pdf may need a temporary decrypted file (kept in app-private, OS-encrypted storage and deleted on close). Per-resource on-the-fly decryption is the roadmap fix. Detail: ENCRYPTION.md.

Honest ceiling

Any client-side decryption can ultimately be defeated — plaintext exists in memory at read time. The MVP measures make this a strong deterrent-grade protection; the final increment is exactly what paid Readium LCP provides. Full facts & citations: ENCRYPTION.md.

Observability & Tracking

Two audiences, one mechanism. Prometheus + Grafana, woven into every service as it's built — like logging. Operational metrics come almost free from Spring Boot Actuator + Micrometer; business KPIs are thin counters incremented at the same points we already log a domain event.

Definition of done — not a phase

A service isn't "done" until its metrics are emitted, in the same PR as its logs. The Prometheus scrape + Grafana instance are stood up once, early (at platform bring-up); only the dashboard panels evolve. Observability is never a trailing "phase 8."

Ops dashboard

Traffic (req/s), latency p50/p95/p99, error rates, JVM + Mongo health, service availability. Mostly automatic.

Business dashboard

Logins, content opens, downloads, denials by reason, reading time — split by content type / org.

The boundary

Deep per-entity BI (top-N titles, cohorts, per-user journeys) is the roadmap's Redshift + Power BI pipeline — not Prometheus.

Why Readium

The Readium toolkit is free (BSD-3) and brings text-to-speech, in-book search, highlights, custom fonts, and accessibility for free — so several roadmap reading features are low-cost later. The main effort is the native reader view (a React Native Fabric component with two-way events, built per platform) — the single largest client task and a known risk.

Engineering & API Reference

The build-level view — the REST APIs each service exposes and the Spring Cloud patterns that implement them. The full how-to (code, indexes, docker-compose, seeding, tests) lives in IMPLEMENTATION.md.

Baseline

☕Services

Java 17 + Spring Boot 3 (MVC for services, WebFlux for the Gateway). Discovery via Eureka, config via Config Server, service calls via OpenFeign.

🔑Tokens

jjwt signs/validates the access JWT; BCrypt hashes passwords; refresh tokens hashed (SHA-256) in sessions.

🗄️Data

Spring Data MongoDB, DB-per-service; ownership by id arrays (licenseId[]/entitlementId[]) written idempotently with $addToSet; TTL index for sessions.

How to create a service (the recipe)

  1. Scaffold with the starter deps; set spring.application.name = the service id (used by Eureka, Feign, and Gateway routing).
  2. spring.config.import: configserver: to pull config; point spring.data.mongodb.uri at the service's own DB.
  3. Expose health + prometheus on the management port (see Observability).
  4. @Document model → MongoRepository → @Service → @RestController; create indexes; register the route on the Gateway.
  5. Emit this service's business metrics next to its log lines — part of the service's definition of done, same PR, like logging.

Key implementation patterns

API Gateway — validate the access token once, inject identity downstream
// WebFlux GlobalFilter — the single security choke-point
if (path.startsWith("/auth/login") || path.startsWith("/auth/refresh"))
    return chain.filter(exchange);                    // public routes
var claims = jwt.parse(bearer).getPayload();          // 401 if invalid -> client silent-refresh
exchange.mutate().request(r -> r
    .header("X-User-Id",   claims.getSubject())
    .header("X-User-Orgs", orgsCsv)).build();         // downstream services trust these
Provisioning — idempotent reference write ($addToSet)
// grant a license to an org: create the thin doc, then reference it
License lic = licenses.save(new License(isbn, start, end, openAccess));
mongoTemplate.updateFirst(query(where("_id").is(orgId)),
    new Update().addToSet("licenseId", lic.getId()), Organization.class);  // no duplicate id
// (entitlements work the same via user.entitlementId[]). The gate is a READ: resolve ids,
// validate start<=now<=end — no create-race to guard.
React Native — silent refresh on a 401
api.interceptors.response.use(ok, async (err) => {
  if (err.response?.status === 401 && !err.config._retried) {
    err.config._retried = true;
    const { accessToken } = await refresh(await getRefresh());   // POST /auth/refresh
    err.config.headers.Authorization = `Bearer ${accessToken}`;
    return api(err.config);                                      // retry original request
  }
  throw err;
});
Build-time pitfalls

Hash refresh tokens at rest (never raw in the DB or AsyncStorage); the unique index + upsert is what stops double-tap duplicate grants; never put a secret (content key, refresh token) in a JWS; the native reader view (Fabric) is the schedule risk, not the endpoints.

Design Evolution

The MVP was refined through eleven clarification rounds — captured so the reasoning is never lost.

ROUND 1

Content & auth boundaries

Books + journals only (journal → many articles; no chapters); university & catalogue data mocked; files in S3; JWT only, no SAML.

ROUND 2

B2B + reading features

No B2C; a rough DB schema; membership pre-seeded on both sides; browse-all, gate at read/download, open-access bypass; build reading features.

ROUND 3

Auth & DRM narrowed

No SAML/SCIM at all; not implementing concurrent-seat DRM (organization.DRM removed).

ROUND 4

Protection re-scoped

Non-open-access content still needs protection — the earlier "no DRM" meant only concurrent-seat DRM; open-access served plainly.

ROUND 5

Readium research + License layer

Real Readium LCP is paid/gated → not used; our License schema is the protection mechanism; the Readium reading toolkit is free (BSD-3).

ROUND 6

Grants as first-class collections

licenses and entitlements become materialized, time-bounded, lazily-refreshed collections; multi-org; cleanups (dropped audiobook, subscribedIds[], "bought").

ROUND 7

Encryption + online/offline

AES-256-GCM at rest locked in; key over TLS (never in the signed token); online = key per session, offline = key in Keychain/Keystore + local exp.

ROUND 8

Observability

Prometheus + Grafana woven in like logging; Ops + aggregate business KPIs; bounded labels only; deep BI deferred to Redshift.

ROUND 9

Cross-team review + sessions

Folded in engineering-hardening from a peer team's design: access + refresh tokens with multi-device sessions & silent refresh; reader recast as a native Fabric component; whole-file-decrypt limitation documented; publisher collection dropped.

ROUND 10

Inverted license/entitlement ownership

User requirement: store the ids, not the payload. licenses → {isbn, start, end, openAccess}, entitlements → {start, end} (no userId/orgId); the org holds licenseId[], the user holds entitlementId[]. The materialized-grant/upsert model is replaced by reference arrays the gate reads & window-validates ($addToSet for idempotency). Long arrays accepted.

ROUND 11

Services kept separate + KMS write-up

Kept Organization, License, and Entitlement as separate services — each a single-responsibility bounded context with its own database and deploy path. "University" is generalized to Organization (its own service). The read/download gate lives in License, which chains synchronous calls to Organization (subscription + membership) and Entitlement (user validity) — the accepted cost of separate contexts. Key management made explicit: per-file content key wrapped by a master key (secret store → AWS KMS); all keys exist before /access, which only unwraps + releases the key over TLS.

Glossary

License (our meaning)A thin licenses doc ({isbn, start, end, openAccess}, no userId/orgId) an org references via licenseId[], plus the unlock token the License service mints to open non-open-access content.
Entitlement (our meaning)A thin entitlements doc ({start, end}, no userId/orgId) a user references via entitlementId[]; the user must also be active in the org's userList[].
Reference arrays + windowOwnership lives in organization.licenseId[] / user.entitlementId[] (updated via $addToSet); the gate honors a referenced doc only while start ≤ now ≤ end.
Open accessContent readable by anyone logged in, no license/entitlement, served plainly.
OrganizationA University or Library — the licensee and tenant. userList[] is the authoritative membership roster. Its own service (generalized from "University").
Organization / License / Entitlement servicesThe three bounded contexts behind "who may read what": Organization owns the tenant (organization — licenseId[], subscription, userList[]); License owns licenses and runs the read/download gate + unlock; Entitlement owns entitlements. The gate (License) chains calls to Organization + Entitlement.
Readium toolkitThe free, open-source (BSD-3) reading toolkit. Renders EPUB and gives TTS, in-book search, highlights, fonts, and accessibility for free.
Readium LCPReadium's paid DRM (EDRLab agreement + annual cert + confidential liblcp). Not used in the MVP.
OPDSOpen Publication Distribution System — an open standard serving a catalogue as a feed at a URL. Catalog exposes OPDS 2.0.
JWTSigned token issued at login, carried as Bearer, validated at the Gateway; its payload drives the checks.
S3 signed URLA time-limited URL granting temporary access to a private S3 object; used after the checks pass.
Denormalization ("Architecture 1")Embedding content-type fields inside catalogue so reads/search hit one collection (no join), at the cost of duplicated data.
Metrics vs eventsMetrics = aggregates with bounded labels (Prometheus); raw per-user/per-title events go to logs now → a warehouse later.
